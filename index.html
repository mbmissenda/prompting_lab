Prompting Well: Describing What You Want

A hands-on lab. Newer AI models — especially agentic tools that carry out multi-step work — need less clever wording and more clarity about what you want, why, and what "good" looks like. You'll practice describing that clearly, then test it in Claude.

What you'll practice

  • Brief Claude the way you'd brief a capable colleague: the goal and why, the context, what good looks like, the boundaries, and the output.
  • Let Claude interview you when you're not yet sure how to describe what you want.
  • Treat the first response as a draft to steer — and ask for a plan first on bigger jobs.
  • Move from chatting to delegating a whole task, using a Project to hold your course context.
  • Use Claude as a thinking partner, and build a verification habit that lasts.

Principle 1

Start with the goal — and why it matters

The most common problem isn't wording — it's that the goal was never stated. And the single cheapest improvement is adding why. When Claude knows your reasoning, it makes better choices about everything you didn't spell out.

⚠ Under-defined
Create a class activity about hypertension.
Claude has to guess: which students, which concept, five minutes or ninety — and for what purpose?
✓ Goal + why
Create a 20-minute small-group activity for second-year nursing students on the factors that contribute to poorly controlled hypertension. The purpose: on last term's exam, students could list risk factors but couldn't explain how they interact in a real patient. I want them practicing that reasoning, not memorizing another list.
The "why" tells Claude to build for reasoning about interactions — a design decision you didn't have to spell out.

Try it in Claude

Paste both prompts (one at a time, new chat each) into Sonnet 5. Read the two responses side by side.

Which would need less editing? Look for choices Claude made because of the "why" — you'll often see it shape the whole activity.

Then steer it. Send one follow-up:

Add a 3-minute debrief I can lead at the end, with two questions that surface why hypertension stays poorly controlled.

Reflect

In one or two sentences: what do you want to build this term, and why — what problem in student learning is it solving?

Principle 2

Give it the context you already have

Claude doesn't know your course. It doesn't know who your students are, what they learned last week, or how they'll encounter the material — unless you tell it, or hand it your materials.

⚠ No context
Create a discussion question about inclusive teaching.
Acceptable, but generic — it could fit any course at any level.
✓ Situated in the course
I teach an asynchronous graduate course for practicing K-12 educators. This week's module examines Universal Design for Learning. Students already know the basic UDL framework. Write a discussion question that moves beyond defining UDL and requires them to apply it to a realistic classroom problem.
Claude now knows where this sits in the learning sequence.

Same goal, different students. Context doesn't just add detail — it changes the right answer. Here's one learning goal briefed for two very different settings:

● In-person undergraduate
I teach an in-person intro psychology course, mostly first-year students, 35 per section. Goal: students can tell correlation from causation in a news headline. They've had one lecture on it. Design a 15-minute in-class activity with a lot of structure — many haven't done group work in college yet. Include what I say to launch it and how I'll know, by the end, who got it.
Structure, live facilitation, and a quick in-room check for understanding.
● Online asynchronous graduate
I teach an asynchronous online graduate research methods course; most students work full-time and log in at different times during the week. Goal: students can tell correlation from causation in published research claims. Design a week-long discussion activity that works without me present: an initial post, a structured peer reply, and a prompt that forces them to engage with a study from their own field.
No live facilitator, staggered participation, and professional relevance for adult learners.

Try it in Claude — add your own materials, and an example

Start a new chat and attach a real file — a learning objective, a rubric, or case materials — and ask Claude to work from it:

Here are the learning objectives and case materials for my unit. Identify where the current case requires clinical reasoning and where students could answer primarily through recall.

Show, don't just tell. If you have something you already like, attach it as a model. An example often communicates your standard better than a paragraph of description:

Attached is a discussion question from Module 3 that worked well — students had to take a position and defend it with the reading. Write one for Module 5 (topic: health literacy) that matches that level of rigor and structure. Don't copy its content.

Before you attach student work — privacy and FERPA

  • Remove every identifier, not just names: IDs, emails, dates, locations, and details a classmate would recognize.
  • Pro is a consumer plan. Open Claude's Settings → Privacy and review the data settings, including whether your chats may be used to improve models.
  • Follow your institution's AI and data-use policy. When in doubt, write a realistic composite sample instead.

Self-check

Claude gives you a flat, generic response. Before you rewrite your prompt, what's the first thing to check?

Show a good answer
Whether you gave it enough context. Generic in, generic out. Name the course, the level, the modality (in-person or asynchronous), what students already know, and the rigor you expect — or attach materials and an example that carry that context for you.
Principle 3

Describe what good looks like — and the boundaries

Tell Claude how you will judge the result. Success criteria and boundaries are where your pedagogical decisions live. Claude doesn't make those calls; you do.

⚠ No criteria
Create a classroom activity comparing two interpretations of Frankenstein.
Claude picks the time, format, and even the goal for you.
✓ Success criteria + boundaries
Create a classroom activity comparing two interpretations of Frankenstein for undergraduate English majors. I have 25 minutes; students work in groups of four. They've read the novel but no literary criticism yet. It succeeds if: every group cites specific passages, and the class ends in real disagreement between two plausible readings rather than one correct answer. Boundaries: no lecture, no worksheet.
Claude can now check its own draft against the criteria you'll use.

Try it in Claude — then steer it

Run the strong prompt in Sonnet 5. Read it against your own success criteria — would it pass?

Then push on the result:

Keep the disagreement, but make the two interpretations closer so the choice is genuinely hard rather than obvious.

Reflect

Finish this sentence for your own activity: "It succeeds if students..." Then name one boundary that keeps them from taking a shortcut.

Principle 4

Say what the output should be — and where it's going

A response can be accurate and still useless if it's the wrong shape. Describe the product you need and its destination — a Canvas page, a slide, a handout — and the student-facing requirements that come with it.

⚠ Shape unspecified
Help me teach pulmonary embolism.
You might get pages of explanation you didn't need.
✓ Product defined
Give me three five-minute case variations for an in-person lecture. Each case should change one clinically meaningful variable. After each case, give one question to ask the class and a short instructor note explaining the reasoning I should listen for.
Clear format, clear count, clear purpose for each piece.

Try it in Claude — then reshape only the output

Run the strong prompt. Then send one follow-up that changes only the output:

Put that into a table with four columns: scenario, common novice interpretation, more sophisticated interpretation, and a question that moves the student forward.

You didn't restate the task — you just reshaped the product.

Student-facing requirements worth naming in the output: reading level, length, accessibility (headings, plain language, alt text for images, no meaning carried by color alone), and paste-ready formatting for your LMS.

Self-check — match the output to the setting

Your course is asynchronous in Canvas, not a live lecture. How might you describe the output differently?

Show one good answer
Ask for student-facing text you can reveal in stages, with a separate instructor key. E.g., "Create three progressive versions of this case I can release one at a time as Canvas pages. Give the student-facing text first, written for graduate learners, with clear headings. Then a separate instructor key identifying the reasoning expected at each stage."
Principle 5 · New

Not sure how to describe it? Let Claude interview you

Describing clearly what you want is the core skill — and it's hard to do cold. You don't have to. Ask Claude to draw it out of you before it starts. This is often the fastest route to a good brief, and it models the kind of questioning you'd want from a good collaborator.

⚠ Guess and revise
Make me a rubric for my capstone paper.
Claude invents criteria; you spend three rounds correcting them.
✓ Interview first
I need a rubric for my capstone paper. Before you draft anything, ask me up to five questions about my students, the assignment's goals, and what separates strong from weak work in my field. Ask them one at a time and wait for my answers.
Your answers become the brief. You describe what you want by responding, not by composing.

Try it in Claude

Run the "interview first" prompt with a real assignment of yours. Answer the questions honestly — "I'm not sure" is a useful answer too.

Notice which questions you hadn't thought to answer. Those gaps are exactly what your earlier prompts were missing.

Principle 6

Steer the response — and check in before bigger jobs

For quick requests, don't spend twenty minutes perfecting a prompt. Give Claude enough to begin, then steer. But the longer and more autonomous the task, the more your up-front description matters — an agent may take many steps before you see anything. So for bigger jobs, ask for a plan first.

Quick work: steer in small turns

Start with any case Claude gave you above, then send these in sequence, one message at a time:

Make the clinical ambiguity greater.
The ambiguity is better, but this presentation is clinically unlikely. Preserve the ambiguity while making the case realistic for outpatient primary care.
Good. Now remove the diagnostic label so students have to infer it.

Each turn shapes the result a little more. For short tasks, steering beats one giant prompt.

Bigger work: build in a checkpoint

Add a line like this to any multi-part request (a full module, a set of cases, a revised syllabus section):

Before you build anything, show me a short plan: what you'll create, in what order, and any assumptions you're making about my students. Wait for my go-ahead.

Correcting a plan takes one message. Correcting a finished module takes many.

Principle 7 · New

From chat to delegation

Agentic tools don't just answer — they carry out a job: reading your files, drafting several pieces, checking their own work, and handing back a finished product. Delegating well uses everything above, plus three things chat rarely needs: a clear deliverable, a self-check, and instructions for when to stop and flag a problem.

⚠ A request
Write my Week 4 overview page.
No sources, no audience, no finish line.
✓ A delegation brief
Build a Canvas-ready overview page for Week 4 using the attached syllabus and readings. Audience: asynchronous online graduate students, mostly working professionals. Done looks like: under 400 words; states the week's two learning objectives in plain language; tells students what to do and in what order; ends with one reflection prompt tied to the objectives. Requirements: accessible headings, no meaning carried by color alone, paste-ready for Canvas. Before you hand it back, check it against the learning objectives and list anything you couldn't verify from the attached files. If the readings and syllabus conflict, stop and ask me.
Deliverable, audience, finish line, self-check, and when to ask.

Set up a Project — context you give once

On Pro, a Project holds files and instructions that every chat inside it can use. It's the practical version of "context engineering": instead of re-explaining your course each time, you curate it once.

1. In Claude, create a Project named for one real course.

2. Add your syllabus, learning objectives, one rubric, and one example of work you consider strong (de-identified).

3. In the Project's instructions, describe your students and your standards in a few sentences, for example:

These are asynchronous online graduate students in integrative health, most working full-time. Write student-facing material in plain, warm, professional language at a graduate level. Prioritize applying ideas over recalling them. Flag any factual claim you aren't confident about.

4. Run the delegation brief above inside the Project, with less context in the prompt itself. Compare the result to your incognito runs.

A few well-chosen files beat a folder dump. Curate what Claude should use, and remove anything outdated.

Reflect

What's one recurring task you could delegate this way? What would "done" look like, and when should Claude stop and ask you?

Principle 8

Ask Claude to help you think, not just produce

One of the most valuable moves is asking Claude to critique your thinking rather than generate a product. You don't need elaborate role-play — what matters is the stance you ask for: skeptical, specific, and holding off on rewriting.

⚠ Produce only
Improve this assignment.
✓ Think with you
Read this assignment skeptically, the way an experienced instructional designer would. Identify three assumptions I appear to be making about what students already know. Do not rewrite anything yet.

More moves worth trying — copy any into Claude with your own materials attached:

Where could a student reach the correct answer in this case using superficial pattern recognition rather than real reasoning?
I think this assignment assesses synthesis. Challenge that assumption. Give evidence for and against my interpretation.
Read this prompt as a first-year undergraduate would, then as a working professional in an online graduate program. Where would each get confused or take a shortcut?

Where the deeper model earns its keep

Critique and "challenge my assumptions" tasks are where Opus 4.8 with more thinking tends to shine. If you have usage to spare, run one prompt above in both models and compare how each pushes back.

Reflect

Write one prompt that asks Claude to critique something you're designing — without rewriting it.

Principle 9

Verification is a habit that decays — build it now

Better prompting doesn't remove Claude's limits. A polished response can still be wrong, and plausible errors are the hard ones to catch. Your judgment moves upstream: you define the problem, supply the disciplinary context, and decide what belongs in your teaching.

Here is the trap, and it's well documented: once responses keep passing your checks, you stop running them. Verification lapses precisely because the model looks reliable. If you let that happen here, you carry a miscalibrated sense of AI accuracy into agentic tools — where output arrives faster than you can read it and a single unchecked error compounds downstream.

Make errors easier to spot. Tell Claude up front that uncertainty is welcome: "If you're not sure about a fact or can't verify a source, say so rather than guessing." A model that is allowed to say "I don't know" is easier to trust — and to check.

Start with the check that matters most to you — for most people, at first, that's made-up ("hallucinated") facts and citations. Tap each item as you verify the last response you got:

  • Every factual claim and citation is verified against a real source — not just plausible-sounding.
  • Any clinical, technical, or procedural detail is realistic and correct.
  • Disagreement among experts or scholars hasn't been flattened into one view.
  • Cultural or disciplinary context hasn't been stripped away.
  • The activity actually assesses the learning objective — not just something engaging.
  • It is accessible and appropriate for these students (level, modality, reading load).

Make it a practice, not a one-time check

1. Keep a short personal checklist of what must be true in your field, and run it by hand every time — even when you're sure it will pass.

2. Expect it to stop feeling necessary. When checking starts to feel like a formality, that's the decay — not proof the model is safe. Catching yourself in that slip is the skill.

3. Re-check adversarially. Paste your draft into a fresh chat and ask Claude to find problems — always before anything reaches students:

You are a skeptical fact-checker. Here is a draft I plan to give students. List every factual or citation claim you cannot verify, and every place a specialist might disagree. Do not rewrite it — only find problems.

Closing reflection — write your checklist

Name the two or three checks that must pass before anything you make with AI reaches your students. Keep it — and expect to have to remind yourself to run it.

Takeaway

You don't need a secret language — you need to know what you want

Brief Claude as you would a capable colleague who knows a great deal about the world but nothing about your course: the goal and why, the context, what good looks like, the boundaries, and the output. If you can't articulate it yet, let Claude interview you. Ask for a plan on bigger jobs. Then inspect what comes back and steer it — and never skip the check before it reaches students.
