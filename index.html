Prompting in a New Generation of AI

A hands-on lab. You'll build prompts, compare weak and strong versions, and test your work in Claude — across models and effort levels — to see how clear communication shapes what you get back.

What you'll practice

  • Communicate a task to Claude the way you'd brief a capable colleague — using Task, Context, Constraints, Output.
  • Run the same prompt in different models (Sonnet 5, Opus 4.8) and effort levels (extended thinking on/off) and notice the difference.
  • Treat the first response as a starting point and refine through conversation.
  • Use Claude as a thinking partner — to critique, compare, and stress-test your own design.

Before you begin — open Claude in another tab

Go to claude.ai and keep it open beside this page. Throughout the lab you'll copy prompts from here and paste them there. Watch for two controls in Claude's message box:

Model selector

Switch between Sonnet 5 (fast, capable, great default) and Opus 4.8 (deepest reasoning). Try the same prompt in each.

Extended thinking / effort

Toggle extended thinking on to let Claude reason more before answering. Compare a reply with it off vs. on.

Model names and controls update over time — use whichever current models and effort settings your account offers. The skill is the comparison, not the specific label.

Principle 1

Start with the task

The most common problem with a prompt isn't a broken formula — it's that the task was never defined. Tell Claude what you're actually trying to accomplish.

⚠ Under-defined
Create a class activity about hypertension.
Claude has to guess: which students, which concept, five minutes or ninety?
✓ Clear task
Create a 20-minute small-group activity for second-year nursing students learning to recognize factors that contribute to poorly controlled hypertension.
Now Claude understands the work: who, what, and how long.

Try it in Claude

Paste both prompts (one at a time, new chat each) into Sonnet 5. Read the two responses side by side.

Which one would need less editing before you could use it? Notice how much of the "improvement" came from you defining the task, not from any special wording.

Reflect

In one sentence, what is the task for an activity you actually need to build this term?

Principle 2

Give it the context you already have

Claude doesn't know your course. It doesn't know what students learned last week, your terminology, or the rigor you expect — unless you tell it, or hand it your materials.

⚠ No context
Create a discussion question about inclusive teaching.
Acceptable, but generic — it could fit any course at any level.
✓ Situated in the course
I teach an asynchronous graduate course for practicing K-12 educators. This week's module examines Universal Design for Learning. Students already know the basic UDL framework. Write a discussion question that moves beyond defining UDL and requires them to apply it to a realistic classroom problem.
Claude now knows where this sits in the learning sequence.

Try it in Claude — and add your own materials

Run the strong prompt. Then start a new chat and attach a real file — a learning objective, a rubric, or an anonymized sample of student work — and ask Claude to work from it. For example:

Here are the learning objectives and case materials for my unit. Identify where the current case requires clinical reasoning and where students could answer primarily through recall.

This is a very different use of AI than "make my assignment better." You've supplied the intellectual environment Claude should work in.

Self-check

Claude gives you a flat, generic response. Before you rewrite your prompt, what's the first thing to check?

Show a good answer
Whether you gave it enough context. Generic in, generic out. Name the course, the level, what students already know, and the rigor you expect — or attach the materials that carry that context for you.
Principle 3

Identify the constraints

Constraints tell Claude what boundaries matter — and they're where your pedagogical decisions live. Claude doesn't make those calls; you do.

⚠ No boundaries
Create a classroom activity comparing two interpretations of Frankenstein.
Claude picks the time, format, and even the goal for you.
✓ Boundaries that carry your design
Create a classroom activity comparing two interpretations of Frankenstein. I have 25 minutes; students work in groups of four. They've read the novel but no literary criticism yet. No lecture or worksheet. The activity should require students to cite passages from the text and end in disagreement between plausible interpretations rather than one correct answer.
Every constraint encodes a teaching choice you made.

Try it in Claude — then compare models

Run the strong prompt in Sonnet 5. Then run the exact same prompt in Opus 4.8.

Where do they differ? A task with genuine intellectual tension (like "end in disagreement") is a good place to feel what the deeper-reasoning model adds — and whether it's worth it for this task.

Reflect

Constraints can cover time, student level, technology, accessibility, disciplinary standards, class size, assessment format — or what you specifically don't want. Name one constraint you'd add to keep students from taking a shortcut.

Principle 4

Say what a useful output looks like

A response can be completely accurate and completely useless if it's the wrong shape. Describe the product you need — it saves editing and forces you to decide what you actually want.

⚠ Shape unspecified
Help me teach pulmonary embolism.
You might get pages of explanation you didn't need.
✓ Product defined
Give me three five-minute case variations for an in-person lecture. Each case should change one clinically meaningful variable. After each case, give one question to ask the class and a short instructor note explaining the reasoning I should listen for.
Clear format, clear count, clear purpose for each piece.

Try it in Claude

Run the strong prompt. Then, in the same chat, change only the output: "Put that into a table with four columns: scenario, common novice interpretation, more sophisticated interpretation, and a question that moves the student forward."

Notice you didn't restate the whole task — you just reshaped the output. That's the conversation working for you.

Self-check — match the output to the setting

Your course is asynchronous in Canvas, not a live lecture. How might you describe the output differently?

Show one good answer
Ask for student-facing text you can reveal in stages, with a separate instructor key. E.g., "Create three progressive versions of this case I can reveal sequentially in Canvas. Give the student-facing case text first, then a separate instructor key identifying the reasoning expected at each stage."

Principle 5

The first response is the beginning

A prompt doesn't have to contain everything. Claude works conversationally — review what it produces, name what's wrong, and direct it. Don't spend twenty minutes crafting a "perfect" prompt; give it enough to begin, then steer.

Try a refinement chain in Claude

Start with any case Claude gave you above, then send these in sequence, one message at a time:

Make the clinical ambiguity greater.
The ambiguity is better, but this presentation is clinically unlikely. Preserve the ambiguity while making the case realistic for outpatient primary care.
Good. Now remove the diagnostic label so students have to infer it.

Each turn shapes the result a little more. This is the recommended way to work — not one giant prompt.

Principle 6

Ask Claude to help you think, not just produce

As models get stronger, one of the most valuable moves is asking Claude to critique your thinking rather than generate a product. Use it as a critic, simulator, or alternative perspective.

⚠ Produce only
Improve this assignment.
✓ Think with you
Read this assignment as a skeptical instructional designer. Identify three assumptions I appear to be making about what students already know. Do not rewrite anything yet.

More moves worth trying — copy any into Claude with your own materials attached:

Examine this case as an experienced educator in my field. Where could a student reach the correct answer using superficial pattern recognition rather than real reasoning?
I think this assignment assesses synthesis. Challenge that assumption. Give evidence for and against my interpretation.

Where the deeper model earns its keep

Critique and "challenge my assumptions" tasks are exactly where Opus 4.8 with extended thinking tends to shine. Run one of the prompts above in Sonnet 5, then in Opus 4.8 with thinking on, and compare how each pushes back.

Reflect

Write one prompt that asks Claude to critique something you're designing — without rewriting it.

Principle 7

Your expertise still decides

Better prompting doesn't remove Claude's limits. A polished response can still be wrong — and plausible errors are the hard ones to catch. Your judgment moves upstream: you define the problem, supply the disciplinary context, and evaluate what belongs in your teaching.

Tap each item as you check the last response you got:

  • Every factual claim and citation is verified, not just plausible-sounding.
  • Any clinical, technical, or procedural detail is realistic and correct.
  • Disagreement among experts or scholars hasn't been flattened into one view.
  • Cultural or disciplinary context hasn't been stripped away.
  • The activity actually assesses the learning objective — not just something engaging.

Closing reflection

The real faculty skill is broader than prompting: it's formulating a problem clearly enough that a capable collaborator — human or AI — could understand the intellectual work. What's one place in your teaching you'll use this?

Takeaway

You don't need a secret language

Explain the work as you would to a capable collaborator who knows a great deal about the world but very little about your particular course. Give it the task, the context, the constraints, and the output you need. Then inspect what it gives you and continue the conversation — asking it to critique, compare, challenge, and analyze, not just generate.
